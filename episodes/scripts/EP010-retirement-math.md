Here's how retirement was supposed to work. For most of the 20th century it stood on three legs. A pension from your employer. Social Security from the government. And your own savings on top. Three legs, one stool. You only needed your savings to cover the gap the other two left behind.

[VISUAL: DOODLE — a three-legged stool, legs labeled Pension / Social Security / Savings]
Now watch what happens to the stool. Because two of those three legs have been quietly sawed off, and we've been pretending the stool still stands.

[VISUAL: SCREENSHOT — full-page, a source on pension decline; highlight the key stat]
Start with the pension. In 1980, roughly 6 in 10 private-sector workers with a retirement plan had a traditional pension — a guaranteed check for life. Today it's fewer than 1 in 10. The guaranteed check didn't get smaller. It disappeared.

[VISUAL: CHART — line, pension coverage falling from ~60% to under 10%]
In its place, your employer handed you a 401(k) and wished you luck. And a 401(k) is not a pension. A pension is a promise. A 401(k) is a bet — on the market, on your own discipline, and on you somehow knowing how to turn a pile of money into forty years of paychecks.

So the first leg became your problem. Now the second leg — Social Security — the one everybody assumes will catch them.

[VISUAL: SCREENSHOT — full-page SSA page; highlight the replacement-rate line]
Here's the part they don't put on a billboard. Social Security was designed to replace only about 40% of your pre-retirement income. Forty percent. If you earned sixty thousand dollars a year, you're supposed to live on about twenty-four thousand. Try cutting your own life by 60% and see how it feels.

[VISUAL: CHALKBOARD — "You had: $60,000 → You get: ~$24,000"]
Financial planners will tell you that you actually need about 70 to 80% of your old income to keep the same life. Social Security hands you 40. [VISUAL: CHARACTER — host at a chalkboard, pointing]
This is where we meet the most famous rule in retirement: the 4% rule. The idea is simple. In your first year of retirement, you withdraw 4% of your savings. Every year after, you adjust for inflation. Do that, and historically your money had a good chance of lasting about 30 years. Flip the rule around and it tells you exactly how big the pile needs to be.

If you want forty thousand dollars a year from your savings, you divide by four percent — and you need one million dollars. Not to be rich. Just to generate forty thousand dollars a year. To replace a single, very ordinary American income, the math quietly asks you for a seven-figure fortune.
